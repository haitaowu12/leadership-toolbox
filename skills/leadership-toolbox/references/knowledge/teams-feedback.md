## Reusable knowledge synthesis

The following is an original editorial decision aid built from the source distinctions below. It is not a validated diagnostic instrument or a claim that one intervention has been experimentally optimized.

### Diagnose the missing coordination link

Start with one recent episode and reconstruct the sequence: who knew what, when the plan changed, who held authority, what the recipient understood, and which dependency failed. Do not begin by assigning a communication personality.

- If the same team is acting from different versions of today's plan, use L06. The output needs a changed action, affected people, and a checkpoint.
- If work crosses an ownership boundary, use L14. The output needs acceptance plus the recipient's next action and contingency, not merely a sent message.
- If repeated friction comes from incompatible operating expectations, use L15. The output needs an ongoing rule that members can actually follow.
- If everyone understands the request but cannot resource it, use L09 or the legitimate priority owner. Repeating the instruction is unlikely to supply capacity.

This routing distinguishes taskwork from teamwork. A sound norm for disagreeing cannot compensate for an infeasible technical plan; a technically sound plan can still fail at an interface. E23, E24 and B21 support that distinction. The four-way route above is this project's proposed application.

### Feedback is information for action

E20's attention account explains why an accurate observation can still be unhelpful: attention may move from changing the task to defending the self. For L03, therefore, distinguish the work standard, the observed episode, the interpretation, and what the recipient can do next. Asking about intent should discover context, not demand a confession or make the giver's interpretation unfalsifiable.

If the person already knows the gap, ask what blocks action. Lack of skill suggests L12, competing demands L09, and contested facts L30/L11. One practical check is whether the recipient can name a feasible next attempt in their own words. Agreement with the manager and a pleasant reaction do not establish learning. This proposed sequence is not a trial-tested SBI/SBII enhancement.

### Participation, interpersonal risk and influence are distinct

A useful interview distinction is: could this person contribute, did they expect a penalty for contributing, and could the contribution affect anything? These are editorial questions, not a three-factor psychological scale.

L05 can improve access to a turn; L18 can preserve independent preference before aggregation. Neither makes a risky disclosure safe. Ask about an actual recent objection and what happened afterward, rather than accepting “we have an open culture.” Explain decision boundaries before inviting input; provide a private route when a public one is unsafe. Track whether concerns receive an intelligible disposition without reprisal. Do not interpret silence as agreement or demand personal disclosure to demonstrate participation.

The safety literature treats interpersonal consequences as central, and still identifies a gap in experimental evidence for specific ways to create safety (E21–E22). These findings justify a safeguard, not a promise that a facilitation exercise changes climate.

### Conflict is a clue, not a performance target

Ask whether the disagreement concerns evidence or task choices, work allocation and procedure, damaged relationships, or incompatible resource claims. Categories can overlap; a participant's account is not a diagnosis of someone else's motives. E25 finds no overall task-conflict/performance benefit. Do not manufacture dissent to raise a conflict score.

For L04, identify what is genuinely negotiable, what each party values, and whether several issues can be traded without breaching constraints. A disputed factual claim needs verification before bargaining. A process dispute may need role clarification or a charter. Retaliation, rights, or immediate safety needs an appropriate protective process. B20 adds the structural insight that incentives, dependencies and ambiguous jurisdictions can produce conflict without bad intent. National stereotypes in older teaching material should not be turned into predictions about an individual; ask about their preferred process.

### Listening establishes an account; repair establishes conduct

L30 is useful when the missing input is the other person's account. Show attention, invite correction, and test what you understood. Speaker experience matters, but a corrected account must still be distinguished from independently verified fact. Feeling heard also does not authorize unlimited disclosure or an outcome the listener cannot deliver. E26 motivates attention to the speaker's experience while warning against treating favorable self-report as proof of performance.

L32 applies once one's contribution is established. E27 gives bounded evidence about apology wording, not a license to optimize someone into forgiveness. Commit only to a correction that can be resourced, name the follow-through, and respect the affected person's control over further contact. Genuine repair may require changed scheduling, restored access, corrected records or other concrete action; select the action from the actual harm and authorized remit, not a mandatory formula.

### Review usefulness, not ceremony

For each method, distinguish fidelity (did the proposed procedure happen?), immediate output (what was clarified or accepted?), later outcome (what changed at the next relevant episode?), and burden/harm (what became harder or less safe?). The measures proposed below are local learning signals, not validated scales, universal targets, or causal proof.


# Research and reading notes

## E20

### Feedback intervention effects and adverse outcomes

**Bibliography:** Kluger, A. N., & DeNisi, A. (1996). The effects of feedback interventions on performance: A historical review, a meta-analysis, and a preliminary feedback intervention theory. *Psychological Bulletin, 119*(2), 254–284. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254); [inspected primary article PDF](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf).

**Access:** Full text inspected, especially pp. 254–258 and 275–277: construct boundaries, inclusion rules, pooled findings, theory and limitations.

**Evidence note:** Across 131 eligible papers, 607 effects and 23,663 observations (12,652 participants), mean performance effect was d=.41; over 38% of effects were negative. Removing a concentrated set of experiments still left 33% negative. These are effect estimates, not “38% of employees harmed.” The proposed mechanism is attention shifting toward self-related concerns and away from the task. The theory was preliminary; attention was not directly measured in these moderator analyses. Dependencies among effects, selective eligible literature and heterogeneous tasks limit prediction for a particular workplace. This evaluates feedback interventions broadly, not SBI/SBII. L03 should check actionable task understanding and later behavior, not count feedback delivered.

**Reuse:** Original summary only; copyrighted article, no bundled paper, figure or instrument.

## E21

### Psychological safety and learning in real work teams

**Bibliography:** Edmondson, A. (1999). Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams. *Administrative Science Quarterly, 44*(2), 350–383. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999); [Harvard record](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/13a7b031-0fdd-45ec-a7e0-2b80e2bc679f); [inspected primary PDF hosted at MIT](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Organizational_Learning_and_Change/Edmondson_1999_Psychological_safety.pdf).

**Access:** Full text inspected: construct/learning definitions, study methods and results, discussion, pp. 378–379 limitations.

**Evidence note:** Multimethod field study of 51 teams in one manufacturing company found psychological safety associated with learning behavior; incremental team-efficacy results were mixed across self- and observer-rated learning outcomes. Learning behavior statistically mediated safety–performance relationships. The cross-sectional survey cannot establish causality; a small, single-company sample and overlap with related constructs constrain inference. Cohesiveness and agreeable affect are not equivalent to safety for interpersonal risk. Supports attention to help seeking and error discussion in L05/L06/L15/L30; does not test these scripts. Never use cheerful participation or confidence as proof of safe dissent.

**Reuse:** Summarize and cite; do not repackage the published survey as an unrestricted instrument.

## E22

### Psychological-safety synthesis, including intervention gaps

**Bibliography:** Edmondson, A. C., & Bransby, D. P. (2023). Psychological safety comes of age: Observed themes in an established literature. *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior, 10*, 55–78. [DOI/publisher](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-120920-055217); [inspected full PDF](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/pdf/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-120920-055217).

**Access:** Full text inspected: search/coding, pp. 64–65 learning/voice, pp. 71–73 research gaps and practice implications.

**Evidence note:** Systematic search and thematic synthesis of 185 empirical articles published 2013–2021, not a pooled-effect meta-analysis. Most were quantitative and many cross-sectional. The review links safety with learning, voice, work experience and leadership, but also reports contingent/adverse pathways and emphasizes the shortage of causal tests of concrete climate-building interventions. Safer discussion of mistakes and greater sharing of harmless good news need not respond identically. For L05/L18, separately inspect access to participation, interpersonal consequences and use of input. Do not claim participation counts demonstrate psychological safety or prescribe a universal safety-building script.

**Reuse:** Publisher declares CC BY 4.0, with separate third-party material caveats. This deliverable uses original summaries and no copied material.

## E23

### Structured handoffs: a multicomponent clinical program
Starmer et al. (2014), *New England Journal of Medicine*, 371, 1803–1812. [Publisher/DOI](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsa1405556); [inspected author-uploaded full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/268524846_Changes_in_Medical_Errors_after_Implementation_of_a_Handoff_Program); [institutional record](https://digitalcommons.wustl.edu/open_access_pubs/3490/).

**Read:** Complete article OCR, relevant design/results/limitations, pp. 1804–1811. Supplements and complete PDF not inspected.

Nine pediatric programs contributed 10,740 admissions. Errors fell from 24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions; preventable adverse events from 4.7 to 3.3. Pre/post windows matched season. Incident adjudicators were blinded to period; collectors and handoff-quality assessors were not. Process measures improved across sites while three sites lacked significant error reductions. The nonrandomized bundle cannot isolate check-back or establish office-work effects.

**Application:** Observe receiver understanding, downstream omissions and burden separately. Better documentation alone does not establish better outcomes. Keep qualified clinical processes in place.

**Reuse:** Copyrighted source; no training materials, article or clinical protocol reproduced.

## E24

### Charters interact with task strategy

**Bibliography:** Mathieu, J. E., & Rapp, T. L. (2009). Laying the foundation for successful team performance trajectories: The roles of team charters and performance strategies. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 94*(1), 90–103. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013257); [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19186898/). Correction: (2010), *95*(4), 712, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019852); [correction abstract on author's publication record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/238308368_Correction_to_Mathieu_and_Rapp_2009).

**Access:** Abstract and correction only for the article. A related [APA PsycTests procedure description](https://doi.org/10.1037/t08708-000) was inspected through indexed publisher text; it is not journal methods/results. The author's [reprint route](https://uchuskypack.com/reprints/) requires a request, which was not submitted.

**Evidence note:** Longitudinal business simulation with 32 MBA teams used growth modeling. Higher-quality task strategies predicted better performance, with the highest sustained performance for teams high on both charter and strategy quality. The sample and simulation constrain transfer; the abstract alone cannot settle causal assignment or all confounds. Importantly, the correction reverses two labels in Figure 1: high-charter/low-strategy and low-charter/high-strategy. Avoid copying the original graphic or deriving trajectory details from it. L15 should test an agreement against a real work plan and necessary resources, rather than treating a completed charter as an outcome.

The related procedure record describes individual capacities/preferences and collective coordination arrangements. It adds procedural context, not evidence of random assignment or an isolated charter effect. Check feasibility against actual availability, expertise and authority.

**Reuse:** Original bibliographic and abstract-based summary only.

## E25

### Task conflict is not beneficial on average

**Bibliography:** de Wit, F. R. C., Greer, L. L., & Jehn, K. A. (2012). The paradox of intragroup conflict: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 97*(2), 360–390. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024844); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21842974/); [inspected article text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51572721_The_Paradox_of_Intragroup_Conflict_A_Meta-Analysis).

**Access:** Full article text inspected: study inclusion/coding, results, discussion and limitations (publisher-provided preview on ResearchGate).

**Evidence note:** Meta-analysis of 116 studies, 8,880 groups, 484 effects. Overall corrected correlations with performance were approximately −.01 for task conflict, −.16 for relationship conflict and −.15 for process conflict. Task-conflict relationships varied across study context and outcome; they were more positive where task/relationship conflict were less correlated. These study-level moderators are not causal prescriptions for individual teams; authors warn against ecological inference. For L04/L15, identify the issue and avoid personal escalation while preserving legitimate disagreement. Do not promise that more task conflict improves performance or that simply relabeling conflict makes it constructive.

**Reuse:** Original summary only; no tables or pooled-data reproduction.

## E26

### Perceived listening: useful association, limited causal evidence

**Bibliography:** Kluger, A. N., Lehmann, M., Aguinis, H., Itzchakov, G., Gordoni, G., Zyberaj, J., & Bakaç, C. (2024; online 2023). A meta-analytic systematic review and theory of the effects of perceived listening on work outcomes. *Journal of Business and Psychology, 39*, 295–344. [DOI/full publisher text](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-023-09897-5).

**Access:** Full text inspected: conceptual definition, review/meta-analysis design, results and methodological limitations.

**Evidence note:** Registered review with three-level meta-analyses, 664 effects and 400,020 observations. Perceived listening correlated with work outcomes (mean r=.39); performance association was r=.36, falling to .21 when solely self-report data were excluded. About 75% of literature relied on self-reports; heterogeneous effects and overlap with relationship quality constrain interpretation. Mostly observational data do not establish the benefit of a particular listening script or training package. L30 should solicit correction from the speaker and check subsequent representation of material concerns, while avoiding a manager's self-score or conversation satisfaction as the sole success measure.

**Reuse:** Open-access primary article; retain attribution. No scales, figures or item banks reproduced.

## E27

### Apology structure versus fulfilled repair

**Bibliography:** Lewicki, R. J., Polin, B., & Lount, R. B., Jr. (2016). An exploration of the structure of effective apologies. *Negotiation and Conflict Management Research, 9*(2), 177–196. [Original DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/ncmr.12073); [current journal record](https://ncmr.lps.library.cmu.edu/article/id/264/); [inspected full text](https://ncmr.lps.library.cmu.edu/article/264/galley/267/view/). Current host also assigns DOI 10.34891/9awp-8644.

**Access:** Full text inspected, including both studies, pp. 190–193 discussion and limitations.

**Evidence note:** Two US scenario/wording experiments (333 and 422 participants) assessed perceived apology effectiveness. More-component apologies generally rated better, but component rankings depended on presentation; requesting forgiveness was least important. Competence-related apologies rated more favorably than integrity-related ones. Some component-count comparisons were null; longer wording may confound comparisons. Studies omitted voice/body language and did not establish restored trust in real relationships. L32 should retain responsibility and feasible repair, with fulfillment and recurrence as the review focus. Do not prescribe a rigid six-component checklist or require the injured person to forgive.

**Reuse:** Journal host declares CC BY-NC 4.0. Summarize findings without reproducing protected scenarios, wording or tables in an MIT file.

## B20

### Open textbook: team design and structural conflict

**Bibliography:** Black, J. S., & Bright, D. S. (2019). *Organizational Behavior*. Houston: OpenStax/Rice University. Publication date 5 June 2019; current online edition inspected 5 October 2026; no numbered edition inferred.

**Inspected scope:** Section [9.3, Managing Effective Work Groups](https://openstax.org/books/organizational-behavior/pages/9-3-managing-effective-work-groups); [10.3, Things to Consider When Managing Teams](https://openstax.org/books/organizational-behavior/pages/10-3-things-to-consider-when-managing-teams); [14.2, Causes of Conflict in Organizations](https://openstax.org/books/organizational-behavior/pages/14-2-causes-of-conflict-in-organizations); [14.4, Negotiation Behavior](https://openstax.org/books/organizational-behavior/pages/14-4-negotiation-behavior). Selected sections read, not the whole book.

**Knowledge note:** The sections connect team outcomes with effort, knowledge/skill, task strategy, organizational support and norms. They also explain conflict arising from interdependence, uncertain jurisdiction, incompatible incentives and scarce resources. Negotiation distinguishes dividing an existing resource from developing mutually useful options. These are explanatory teaching frameworks, not tests of this skill's cards. Some cultural examples are broad and dated; use them to prompt questions about process, never to assign national traits.

**Reuse:** Current page footers state CC BY-NC-SA and additional AI-use restrictions, despite legacy in-page CC BY 4.0 figure credits. Link and provide short original analysis only; do not import prose, diagrams, cases, activities or tables into the MIT skill. No assertion that current text has an unrestricted license.

## B21

### Scholarly book chapter: team development must fit the task

**Bibliography:** National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine. (2025). *The Science and Practice of Team Science*. Washington, DC: National Academies Press. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17226/29043); [Chapter 3, Best Practices](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/29043/chapter/5).

**Inspected scope:** Public full chapter text available; selected sections actually inspected: taskwork/teamwork competencies (pp. 41–43), charters (pp. 60–62), evolving team configurations and leadership (pp. 85–88), and best-practice/research-gap summary. No claim to have read the complete 248-page book.

**Knowledge note:** A consensus synthesis integrates research with committee expertise and distinguishes evidence-supported practices from judgment. Charter studies are limited and often student-based; benefits for performance frequently depend on expertise, task strategy or intervening team processes. Collaboration tools and norms need revision as membership and configuration change. Thus L15 should accompany, not replace, a feasible performance plan, and should reopen when dependencies change. Team-science emphasis limits direct generalization to all work.

**Reuse:** Public reading access does not establish a permissive redistribution license. Use original synthesis and citation only; do not bundle chapter text or figures.
