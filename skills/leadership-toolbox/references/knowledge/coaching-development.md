## Reusable thematic knowledge: choosing the needed kind of help

The following is an original synthesis for the interview and routing layer. The proposed diagnostic questions and workflows are editorial designs, not validated scales or quotations.

### Distinguish five different reasons that work is not moving

1. **Missing authority:** the person could perform the work but must repeatedly seek permission. Ask for the last decision that stalled and who could actually authorize it. Route toward L01 only if real decision rights can move.
2. **Missing capacity or workable conditions:** the work cannot fit its time, resources, dependencies, or quality requirements. Ask what would need to stop if this commitment begins. Route to L09 before adding coaching or a stretch assignment. Treat work design as an intervention target, not background noise [E03].
3. **Missing task capability:** an observed attempt shows a teachable gap. Ask what the person can already demonstrate, rather than whether they feel confident. Route to L12; an open question does not supply missing procedural knowledge [B02].
4. **A genuine choice to explore:** the person knows enough to judge options and can choose among them. Route to L02 after clarifying the manager's evaluative role. Do not use a coaching label to disguise a predetermined directive [B01, E01].
5. **A longer development pathway:** the objective requires several experiences and access decisions over time. Route to L28; distinguish the employee's learning work from the manager's obligation to secure opportunities [E05, E06].

These can coexist. The router should ask which prerequisite fails first, rather than assign someone a permanent category such as “unmotivated” or “not ready.” Keep the working diagnosis provisional and revisit it after an observed attempt.

### Explain the mechanism before prescribing the format

Delegation changes decision rights; autonomy support concerns whether the person understands and endorses choices. Responsibility without genuine discretion can therefore miss the intended motivational mechanism [E02]. Coaching proposes a repeated goal–action–review cycle; a good conversation is only the opening event [E07]. Teaching needs a task-specific learning target and evidence of application, not just agreement with an explanation [E04]. A development assignment needs challenge, usable feedback, and time to learn; making it harder indefinitely is not the theory [E06]. These are mechanism-informed hypotheses, not promises that a card works.

### Design a transfer bridge

For a prescribed routine, check safe execution against a criterion. For an adaptable skill such as handling disagreement, inspect the person's reasoning in a different case as well as the original rehearsal. In both, specify where real application can occur and revisit it after a delay. Keep learning, opportunity to apply, observed use, and useful results distinct [E05]. Short-term fluency may obscure later forgetting; begin with enough support for the learner's knowledge, then adapt practice difficulty [B02]. The exact timings and scenarios should follow task risk and opportunity, not a universal learning-style or development-percentage formula.

### Evaluate change without pretending to run a trial

Before acting, name one primary outcome, its observer, and one plausible cost. Afterward ask separately: Was the intervention actually implemented? Did the intended behavior change? Did the result improve? What else changed? A baseline and an independent observation improve interpretability but do not remove confounding. Aggregate effect sizes across coaching, training, and work redesign are not a ranking of these six cards: the designs, relationships, populations, and outcome measures differ. Preserve dissatisfaction, nonresponse, and negative results rather than counting attendance as success [E01–E07, B01].


# Research and reading notes

## E01

### Workplace coaching outcomes and the manager-coach transfer gap

Jones, R. J., Woods, S. A., & Guillaume, Y. R. F. (2016). The effectiveness of workplace coaching: A meta-analysis of learning and performance outcomes from coaching. *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology, 89*(2), 249–277. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/joop.12119); [author manuscript](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/document/16501). Online first 2015. Access: full_text; methods, outcome analysis, practice moderators, limitations.

Seventeen studies, 2,267 participants, heterogeneous workplaces, mostly management. Coaching had a positive overall corrected effect (δ=.36); outcome types differed. Importantly, manager–subordinate and peer coaching were excluded. Internal coaches here were not supervisors. Duration and delivery-format moderation was nonsignificant. Small study counts and incomplete reporting constrain moderator conclusions; no basis for declaring formats equivalent or a universal session count. Supports measuring a defined developmental outcome, not claiming that L02's four questions or L27's peer format are established treatments. Its organizational sample does not remove the relationship/power mismatch when applying findings to a line manager. No article text, instruments, or figures reproduced.

## E02

### Autonomy support is a relationship quality, not simply fewer approvals

Slemp, G. R., Kern, M. L., Patrick, K. J., & Ryan, R. M. (2018). Leader autonomy support in the workplace: A meta-analytic review. *Motivation and Emotion, 42*, 706–724. [Full article and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-018-9698-y). Access: full_text; construct definition, search/inclusion, results, limitations.

Seventy-two studies, 83 samples, N=32,870; mainly cross-sectional perceived-leadership research. Autonomy support was associated with autonomous motivation, well-being, and performance. Performance associations were larger for self-reports (ρ=.35) than other/objective ratings (ρ=.15). Controlled motivation was unrelated; proposed contextual moderators were largely null. Direction and reciprocal causation remain unresolved. The construct combines perspective-taking, reasons, choice, and encouragement of initiative; it is not a test of L01's agreement form or permission-free working. Useful for L01/L02/L28 as construct-level support. Source is CC BY 4.0; this note supplies independent commentary, no scale reproduction.

## E03

### Work redesign can help, but does not always do so

Knight, C., & Parker, S. K. (2021). How work redesign interventions affect performance: An evidence-based model from a systematic review. *Human Relations, 74*(1), 69–104. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018726719865604); [inspected author manuscript](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/336193787_How_work_redesign_interventions_affect_performance_An_evidence-based_model_from_a_systematic_review); [institutional record](https://curate.curtin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/How_work_redesign_interventions_affect_performance_An_evidence-based_model_from_a_systematic_review/31703350). Online first 2019. Access: full_text; selection/quality appraisal, mechanisms, boundary conditions, limitations.

Systematic review of 55 field interventions: 39 positive, 2 negative, 14 mixed performance findings; 38 used randomized or nonrandomized controlled designs. Motivation, faster local response, and learning were proposed pathways, not consistently tested mediators. Evidence was stronger at individual than team/organizational level; Western settings predominated and few studies measured demands. Supports examining actual work characteristics and implementation alongside outcomes in L09/L01. Does not establish that a short capacity conversation reduces overload. The mixed/adverse findings argue for watching workload displacement and system misalignment. Copyright retained; no source figures or tables copied.

## E04

### Training design and the difference between learning and transfer

Lacerenza, C. N., Reyes, D. L., Marlow, S. L., Joseph, D. L., & Salas, E. (2017). Leadership training design, delivery, and implementation: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 102*(12), 1686–1718. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000241); [university-hosted article](https://doerr.rice.edu/sites/g/files/bxs3016/files/2020-03/Leadership%20Training%20Design,%20Delivery,%20and%20Implementation.pdf). Access: full_text; inclusion/design coding, moderator results, limitations.

335 independent employee samples; repeated-measures and experimental designs, not only randomized trials. Aggregate benefits spanned learning, transfer, and results. Needs analysis, feedback, and practice-containing delivery were useful design correlates. Spacing differentiated transfer/results, but not learning; do not convert this into a universal schedule or claim that every spaced course teaches more. Moderators compare programs and can be confounded; unavailable unsuccessful programs could inflate estimates despite publication-bias tests. Supports planned rehearsal and later application checks for L12/L28; it does not validate this particular teach–practice sequence or predict an individual's gain. No copyrighted assessment or training material reproduced.

## E05

### Transfer depends on opportunities, context, and measurement

Blume, B. D., Ford, J. K., Baldwin, T. T., & Huang, J. L. (2010). Transfer of training: A meta-analytic review. *Journal of Management, 36*(4), 1065–1105. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206309352880); [author-uploaded full article](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235975809_Transfer_of_Training_A_Meta-Analytic_Review). Access: full_text; transfer definition, methods/results, open/closed skills, measurement, limitations.

89 studies synthesized predictors of training transfer. Motivation and supportive work environments generally related more strongly to transfer of adaptable/open skills than prescribed/closed skills. Same-source, same-context measurements inflated relationships. Merely using a skill differs from using it effectively, and immediate success differs from maintained application. Brief transfer interventions, including goal setting, had less compelling effects than common advice implies. Broad predictors and sparse subgroups limit specificity. Supports L12/L28's workplace opportunity and delayed demonstration checks, not a causal claim that adding a plan or checkpoint guarantees transfer. Copyright retained; independent notes only.

## E06

### Stretch is a dose to calibrate, not an objective to maximize

DeRue, D. S., & Wellman, N. (2009). Developing leaders via experience: The role of developmental challenge, learning orientation, and feedback availability. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 94*(4), 859–875. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015317); [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ912760). Access: full_text; pp. 863–868 methods/results, 871 limitations.

225 experiences reported by 60 working MBA managers, with supervisor-rated development; multilevel observational analysis. Developmental challenge showed diminishing returns; feedback availability moderated this pattern. Learning orientation's hypothesized nonlinear moderation was not significant (p=.06), nor was cognitive-ability moderation. Retrospective accounts, selected memorable experiences, and a narrow sample restrict causal/general claims. Supports bounded challenge and accessible feedback in L28, not fixed stretch ratios, personality screening, or guarantees of advancement. No scales, figures, or source examples reproduced.

## E07

### GROW appeared in a coaching package, without an isolated GROW test

Grant, A. M., Curtayne, L., & Burton, G. (2009). Executive coaching enhances goal attainment, resilience and workplace well-being: A randomised controlled study. *The Journal of Positive Psychology, 4*(5), 396–407. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760902992456); [author-uploaded article](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/247520307_Executive_coaching_enhances_goal_attainment_resilience_and_workplace_well-being_A_randomised_controlled_study). Access: full_text; intervention, randomized waitlist comparison, outcome-specific tests, limitations.

Analysis included 41 completers from 50 workshop attendees; the immediate group received four professional coaching sessions before the waitlist group. Coaching combined GROW, cognitive-behavioral/solution-focused work, feedback, and between-session action. Self-rated goal attainment improved versus waitlist. Anxiety and stress did not differ significantly in the initial randomized comparison, despite the abstract's broader stress claim. Outcomes were self-reported and organizational change was not measured. This is relevant precedent for L02, but neither isolates GROW's contribution nor tests a brief manager-led conversation. No provider diagrams or session scripts reproduced.

## B01

### Scholarly comparison of helping roles and evaluation

Greif, S. (2025). *The Future of Coaching: Building Bridges Between Science and Practice*. 1st edition. Springer, Cham. XI+300 pp. eISBN 978-3-031-96192-2. [Book and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-96192-2). Access: partial_text: chapter 3, [Comparison of Coaching with Other Methods](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-96192-2_3), pp. 39–50, especially §§3.5–3.6; chapter 7, [Examining Coaching Effectiveness](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-96192-2_7), pp. 185–202, especially §§7.1–7.2.

Useful conceptual distinctions concern management/development conversations, mentoring, coaching, and their role conflicts; evaluation includes prerequisites, process, outcomes, and unwanted effects at different times. Treat this as scholarly synthesis with an identifiable author perspective, not comparative proof that professional coaching dominates mentoring or peer help. Some comparisons rely on small studies. The chapters are public under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0: link and independently discuss ideas; do not import or adapt their tables, questionnaires, or text into the MIT skill. Supports L02/L27/L28 routing and evaluation reasoning.

## B02

### Learning mechanisms and their limits

National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine. (2018). *How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures*. Washington, DC: National Academies Press. 2018 report edition. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17226/24783). Access: partial_text: complete publicly readable chapters 5, [Knowledge and Reasoning](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783/chapter/7), pp. 85–108; and 6, [Motivation to Learn](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783/chapter/8), pp. 109–134; selected substantive sections inspected closely.

This consensus synthesis explains prior knowledge, retrieval, distributed and varied practice, self-explanation, and motivational context. Practice design depends on task and intended retention; interleaving is not universally superior. Self-explanation can be unhelpful with insufficient prior knowledge. Motivation is shaped by agency, purpose, belonging, and experience, not simply a fixed trait. Much supporting research is educational/laboratory-based; workplace transfer needs testing. Useful conceptual grounding for L12/L28 and for switching L02 to instruction when knowledge is missing. Publicly readable is not equivalent to unrestricted source reuse; no passages, diagrams, or instruments reproduced.
