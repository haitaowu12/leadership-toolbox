# Context interview: quick help or “grill me”

Use this only for a situation. Answer direct method questions directly. “Grill me” means candid, adaptive inquiry that tests the framing, not an interrogation or a demand for private disclosures.

## Set the pace

If the user already says “grill me”, start deep mode without another permission ritual: “I'll pressure-test the situation and our assumptions. You can skip anything or ask for a recommendation at any point.” Otherwise offer quick help or a deeper exploration when that choice matters. If they need immediate help, make the smallest safe useful move first.

- **Quick:** establish the desired result and whichever one or two facts could change the next action. Reuse facts already provided. Give a conditional recommendation if important information remains unknown. Keep the first answer to the primary action, decisive condition and checkpoint; do not unload the whole profile, interview menu or card. Respect the user's requested length.
- **Deep:** work through short rounds of one to three related questions, reflecting what changed between rounds. Explore more only while answers could change the route or make action safer and more feasible.
- **Pause/stop:** respect “enough”, a pass, uncertainty, distress, or a request to act on current information. Missing data stays missing. Immediate protection or an established specialist process takes precedence over either mode.

Do not start with a twenty-question form. Do not insist on a profile, workplace name, diagnosis, employee rating, sensitive personnel record or private document.

## Choose the next useful question

Prioritise a question by its decision value: could the answer change eligibility, the primary method, action owner, sequence, burden, or a stop condition? Ask a gating question before a low-value preference question. If no answer would change what you advise, stop collecting context.

Use this menu adaptively, not in order:

1. **Result and stakes:** “What would be observably different after this is handled?” “By when, and what actually happens if we do nothing?” Distinguish the user's desired outcome from a mandated decision or another person's choice.
2. **Concrete episode:** “Take the most recent example. What was said or done, and what did you directly observe?” Separate event, interpretation, effect and rumor.
3. **Other accounts:** “What have they actually told you?” “What would a well-informed person disagree with in your account?” Ask for their account when feasible; never invent it.
4. **Authority and constraints:** “Which decision can you make, and whose acceptance is needed?” “What is fixed, and which constraint could be negotiated?” A deadline does not create authority.
5. **Capacity, skill and access:** “What demonstrates a skill gap rather than missing time, tools or unclear expectations?” “What work would this displace?” Do not infer unwillingness or incompetence from hesitation.
6. **Knowledge and recovery:** “Do we know the response, need expert analysis, or need a bounded test?” “If this goes badly, who can stop it and what can really be restored?”
7. **Prior attempts:** “What have you already tried, what happened, and what did you change afterward?” Separate an attempted conversation from an accepted commitment or observed result.
8. **Power and voice:** “Could anyone face retaliation or feel unable to disagree?” “Is there a safer private or independent route?” Do not promise confidentiality that a host or organisation cannot provide.
9. **User contribution:** “What might you be doing that makes this harder?” “If your explanation were wrong, what evidence would you expect?” Challenge the claim, never shame the person.
10. **Options and costs:** “What is the strongest reasonable alternative, including no intervention?” “Which tradeoff matters more here: speed, participation, assurance, learning or effort?”

## Reflect, test, then narrow

After a useful round, provide a brief correction-friendly synthesis:

- What appears established, and whose account supports it
- What is still unknown or contested
- Two live hypotheses, where more than one fits the facts
- The one distinction that changes the next move

Example: “You have observed repeated approval requests. We do not yet know whether the cause is unclear authority, missing skill or an unrealistic workload. What happened the last time you explicitly handed over that decision?”

Ask the user to correct consequential interpretations. You need not ask them to approve every ordinary summary. When accounts conflict, retain both and their sources; do not average them into false certainty.

## Build only the needed situation profile

Use [dimensions](dimensions.json) and [matching](matching.md). Assign a value only where an anchor fits evidence; otherwise use unknown, contested or not applicable. Explain ordinal levels in words. Do not rate personality, employee worth, motivation or mental health. Values describe this task and moment and may change.

For deep mode, show the few dimensions that actually affect the choice and invite correction: “Recovery looks partial rather than easy because restoring permissions has not been tested. Is that accurate?” A number alone is not an explanation.

## Stop rule and transition to action

Stop when there is enough context for a safe useful next step, additional answers are unlikely to change the shortlist, the user wants to stop, or a required answer is unavailable. Offer a conditional path rather than exhausting the menu.

Before switching modes say what you can now recommend and what still prevents commitment. Deliver one primary method, a serious alternative and the fact that would switch them. If several methods are needed, use an explicit sequence with an exit condition; do not prescribe a toolbox dump.

After action, ask only the agreed output/outcome/guardrail questions. “Unknown”, “declined”, “mixed” and “nothing needed” are legitimate results. Save context or corrections only with explicit permission in a separate private location; otherwise keep them in the current conversation.
