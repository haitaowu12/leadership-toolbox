## Preserved conversation

### Case 1: approval seeking with charged-back mistakes

User:

Use leadership-toolbox. My analyst keeps requesting approval. She's good at the task, but the client charges back any mistakes to her team and I haven't said which exceptions she may decide. Should I coach her to be more confident? Keep it practical.

Assistant:

Start by clarifying decision rights. With undefined exceptions and real chargebacks, seeking approval may be sensible; confidence is an untested explanation.

Use a bounded delegation agreement:
- You specify routine decisions she may own, exceptions you retain, quality limits, and who handles recovery and chargebacks. Confirm you can grant those rights.
- Ask: “Which decisions feel unclear, and what support would let you act safely?” Check willingness, time, and demonstrated ability for the actual scope.
- Rehearse one exception. If capability is missing, provide supervised instruction before widening responsibility.

Propose reviewing the first three eligible cases together: decision quality and timely escalation, plus rework and financial exposure. Pause if the boundary or assurance fails. Fewer approval requests alone wouldn't show improvement.

Coaching becomes useful if she wants to explore a genuine choice after these conditions are workable. The [delegation card](https://github.com/haitaowu12/leadership-toolbox/blob/637a283b9a64c9643ea859b8488497e339d7300c/skills/leadership-toolbox/references/methods/L01-delegation.md) is an editorial synthesis, not a proven confidence intervention.

### Case 1 follow-up: routine authority only

User:

Actually she demonstrated the routine work, but not the risky exceptions. I can give authority for the routine cases only. Give me the next move.

Assistant:

Delegate only the demonstrated routine work; keep risky exceptions with an authorized owner.

Write a one-page boundary together: routine-case criteria, quality standard, decisions she owns, exception triggers, named escalation contact, and recovery/chargeback responsibility. Say: “You can decide these routine cases. If an exception appears, pause and bring it to me; that is the right action.” Confirm her willingness and available time.

Check she can distinguish a routine case from an exception. If she cannot, rehearse that distinction safely before live delegation. Not demonstrating exception handling yet doesn't establish inability.

Propose reviewing the first three routine cases for quality, appropriate escalation, and rework. Pause delegation if classification is unreliable or costs exceed the agreed boundary. Future exception training would require qualified supervision; successful practice still wouldn't grant authority you don't have.

This applies the [L01 delegation card](https://github.com/haitaowu12/leadership-toolbox/blob/637a283b9a64c9643ea859b8488497e339d7300c/skills/leadership-toolbox/references/methods/L01-delegation.md), an editorial procedure rather than a validated intervention.

### Case 2: premortem as a probability estimator

User:

Use leadership-toolbox. I want to use a premortem to estimate whether our launch has a 90% chance of success. Can you help me make that score?

Assistant:

A premortem can't establish a 90% success probability. It generates plausible failure mechanisms; the [L13 evidence boundary](https://github.com/haitaowu12/leadership-toolbox/blob/637a283b9a64c9643ea859b8488497e339d7300c/skills/leadership-toolbox/references/evidence-map.md#l13) explicitly rules out treating it as demonstrated forecasting improvement.

Start with: “What exactly counts as launch success, and by when?” Then separate two outputs:
- Risk discovery: knowledgeable participants independently list failure paths. For each consequential one, record evidence, an observable warning, an owner, and a mitigation or evidence check.
- Probability assessment: use relevant comparable-launch data and an explicit model of your launch's conditions, with uncertainty. If those inputs are unavailable, label the probability unknown rather than turn votes or confidence into 90%.

If you already know the failure paths, prioritize checking the weakest consequential assumption instead of another premortem. Before committing, the launch owner should review unresolved risks and required specialist assessments. Stop any exercise that manufactures reassurance or has nobody able to act on findings.

### Case 3: completed if–then plans without checked opportunities

User:

Use leadership-toolbox. Our team dutifully fills out if-then plans but they don't work. We've never checked whether the cues happen or whether the next system lets them act. What would you do next?

Assistant:

Trace one existing plan through actual work before asking for more plans.

With its owner, check the next few relevant occasions:
1. Did the cue occur?
2. Was it noticed?
3. Was the response possible, including permissions, time, and the receiving system?
4. Did the person act?
5. Did that action help downstream?

Confirm the goal is genuinely chosen. Keep absent cues separate from blocked responses; a completed worksheet tells you neither.

You and the system owner should review the trace after those occasions. Revise an absent/ambiguous cue; route a blocked action to the owner who can fix access or resources. If the downstream route is unclear, walk one real case end-to-end before changing it.

Watch added tracking burden and downstream rework; stop any rule requiring unsafe or impossible action. Success is useful follow-through across eligible opportunities. The [L23 evidence summary](https://github.com/haitaowu12/leadership-toolbox/blob/637a283b9a64c9643ea859b8488497e339d7300c/skills/leadership-toolbox/references/evidence-map.md#l23) supports cautious testing, with uncertain workplace transfer.

### Case 4: the fastest team has different conditions

User:

Use leadership-toolbox. Team A has the fastest response time. I'll copy their process to the rest of the company. They handle simpler cases and can borrow two specialists. Is positive deviance the right tool?

Assistant:

Not yet. Team A hasn't been established as an exceptional performer under comparable constraints: simpler cases and borrowed specialists could explain the speed.

First, have the teams and an analysis owner compare like-for-like case types, identical timing definitions, quality/rework, and all staffing inputs over repeated periods. Include an ordinary-performing comparator. Don't remove needed support just to force comparability.

If an advantage remains, invite the receiving team to observe both the practice and its enabling conditions. Then consider one authorized, recoverable trial using resources that team actually has. If the difference disappears, address the resource or case-routing issue instead of copying a process.

Review end-to-end response time and quality after the agreed trial window; stop if rework or specialist burden crosses agreed limits. The [positive-deviance card](https://github.com/haitaowu12/leadership-toolbox/blob/637a283b9a64c9643ea859b8488497e339d7300c/skills/leadership-toolbox/references/methods/L25-positive-deviance.md) treats exceptional performance as a starting point for inquiry, not proof of a transferable cause.

### Case 5: good charter, unclear integration plan

User:

Use leadership-toolbox. The team agreed on a beautiful charter, but deliveries still miss because no one understands the integration plan. Should we run another agreement workshop?

Assistant:

Clarify the integration plan first. Another agreement workshop is unlikely to fill a task-planning gap.

Ask the integration owner to walk one upcoming delivery through dependencies, sequence, acceptance criteria, available resources, and decision owners. If the technical approach itself is unresolved, get qualified analysis before making new commitments.

If a workable plan exists, use a short brief: each affected person explains their next action, required input, recipient, and escalation trigger. Ask: “Where does this plan stop being executable?” Record gaps and update absent dependency owners.

Propose checking the next integration milestone for missed dependencies, delivery completion, and rework; keep reset-meeting time small. Stop and escalate if the plan requires unavailable resources or authority.

Revisit the charter only if recurring operating norms are also failing. The [L06 brief card](https://github.com/haitaowu12/leadership-toolbox/blob/637a283b9a64c9643ea859b8488497e339d7300c/skills/leadership-toolbox/references/methods/L06-brief-huddle.md) offers an editorial workplace adaptation; adjacent charter and clinical-team evidence doesn't prove this meeting will improve delivery.

This keeps the answer auditable to the material actually read and avoids pretending external-paper inspection, but it falls short of explicitly citing the originating method source. ### What this does and does not establish

This check demonstrates six concrete model responses produced after consulting the pinned skill's knowledge, method, matching and evidence materials. It illustrates mechanism-sensitive routing and one correction-sensitive update.

It does not establish that the skill caused the useful behavior: there is no no-skill baseline or ablation. The prompts are explicitly diagnostic and supply the rival explanation, so they are easier than ambiguous real conversations. The executing model also performed the review. There was one conversational pass, no randomized seeds, no independent raters, no adversarial or paraphrase replication, and no human outcome measurement. Earlier cases and readings remained in context for later cases.

Useful next checks would be:
- An independently reviewed repeat with less-leading prompts, missing or contested accounts, and paired changes to authority, capability, incentives, and resources.
- A no-skill comparison and a knowledge-library ablation, with source-use, interview burden, unsafe assumptions, and correction behavior rated separately.
- If real-world use is later studied, voluntary observation of behavior, outcomes and burden over appropriate opportunities, with privacy and consent safeguards. Model agreement or concise output alone must not be counted as human effectiveness.
