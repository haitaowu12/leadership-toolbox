# Leadership Toolbox: independent forward evaluation

Date: 2026-10-05. All people, organisations, requests and events below are newly invented for this evaluation. The requests and responses contain no private source material.

This is an unseen-from-author, model-executed evaluation, not validated human research. The evaluator generated requests, executed the skill in text and then inspected the resulting responses. No conversations occurred, no proposed actions were taken, and no human outcomes or causal effectiveness are claimed. The author did not supply these cases. Because one evaluator both produced and assessed the responses, this is not blinded scoring or an independent replication.

## 1. Paired delegation case: capacity available

**Request:** “I manage operations at a small museum. Our experienced analyst, Mira, has agreed to own the monthly visitor dashboard but still asks me to approve every chart. I have authority to delegate its format; I retain approval of any published claims. The draft is due Friday 9 October. She knows the data and has four hours available for this work this week. How should I handle the next conversation?”

**Response:** Use L01, an outcome delegation agreement: the task skill, willingness and time are stated; the missing piece appears to be decision boundaries. Mira’s explanation for the approval requests is still missing.

At your next normal check-in, say: “I’d like you to own the draft’s format while I retain approval of published claims. Where is my approval still unclear?” Ask, “Which chart decisions can you comfortably own?” and “What support or exception would make you bring a decision back?”

Together write a short agreement: Mira owns chart selection and layout using the existing data checks; you approve published claims and remain the recovery owner. Confirm that she can explain the boundary before transferring responsibility. Mira owns Friday’s draft; you own resolving any disputed boundary.

The strongest alternative is L09 capacity tradeoff if the four hours are insufficient for the agreed scope. Do not treat her agreement as proof the estimate works.

Review on 9 October: can Mira make the agreed formatting decisions and produce the draft without an unplanned rescue? Baseline frequency of rescue is unknown; use this draft as a first observation. Guardrail: no extra hours or skipped data checks to meet the deadline. Pause and re-scope if either is needed, or authority changes. This is the toolbox’s editorial delegation synthesis, informed by [AHRQ brief guidance](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/team/tools/briefs.html), rather than a validated assessment.

## 2. Paired delegation case: capacity unavailable

**Request:** “I manage operations at a small museum. Our experienced analyst, Mira, has agreed to own the monthly visitor dashboard but still asks me to approve every chart. I have authority to delegate its format; I retain approval of any published claims. The draft is due Friday 9 October. She knows the data and has zero hours available for this work this week. How should I handle the next conversation?”

**Response:** Start with L09 capacity tradeoff. Clearer decision rights will not make Friday’s work feasible when no time is available. The effort needed and which other commitments can change are unknown.

Say: “You have the skill to own this, but there is no time allocated this week. Let’s make the workload decision first.” Ask, “What effort would the smallest acceptable draft take?” and “Which existing commitment could move, and who can approve that change?”

Prepare three concrete options with Mira: move the dashboard deadline, narrow it to the minimum acceptable draft, or release time by moving other work. You own seeking an authorised choice from any other priority owner involved; Mira supplies effort and quality constraints. Leave the dashboard commitment unresolved until a feasible choice is made.

The strongest alternative is L01 delegation once capacity has been released and Mira remains willing. Then clarify format versus publication decisions.

Review the tradeoff by 6 October and feasibility again on 9 October. Signal: can the revised named commitments be completed within the available hours and required checks? Current dashboard capacity is zero; revised capacity remains unknown until agreed. Guardrail: no hidden overtime, displaced mandatory work or unplanned rescue. If no option is feasible, escalate the unresolved demand rather than restating Friday’s commitment. This capacity procedure is an editorial application of resource planning, informed by [AHRQ brief guidance](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/team/tools/briefs.html).

## 3. Paired process case: rollback available

**Request:** “I own the internal archive service at a design cooperative. Two staff members spend about 40 minutes a day filing completed project folders. We want to try a new rule that moves older folders automatically overnight. The data owner has authorised a one-night, 20-folder pilot after a technician verifies the script; both staff have time to check it. We do not yet know whether retrieval will be quicker. A tested rollback can restore every moved folder and its permissions. What should I do before Wednesday’s pilot?”

**Response:** Use L08, a bounded PDSA test, after the technician’s verification and confirmation that rollback covers the actual pilot folders. You have a specific uncertainty, limited scope, time for checks and a stated recovery route. Folder types and normal retrieval times remain unknown.

Before 7 October, agree one practical prediction: “The new location rule will reduce retrieval time for these folders without extra filing or support work.” Ask the two staff, “Which realistic retrieval tasks should we check?” and “What would make us stop and restore the old locations?”

You own a one-page pilot record: the 20 folders, script check, restore owner, prediction and observations. Staff should time the same small set of retrieval tasks before and after the pilot, from search start to opening the correct folder; record baseline as unknown until measured. Keep existing permission checks. The technician owns restoring if required.

Review on 8 October. Signal: retrieval time for the agreed tasks, with departures and alternative explanations recorded. Guardrail: stop and restore on any missing folder, changed access or filing/support effort exceeding the locally agreed cap; set that cap before running. The strongest alternative is L13 premortem plus technical review if rollback is unverified or a failure could escape the 20-folder boundary. One pilot supports a next test decision, not automatic rollout. PDSA is established [IHI improvement guidance](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement/testing-changes); this archive application is editorial.

## 4. Paired process case: rollback unavailable

**Request:** “I own the internal archive service at a design cooperative. Two staff members spend about 40 minutes a day filing completed project folders. We want to try a new rule that moves older folders automatically overnight. The data owner has authorised a one-night, 20-folder pilot after a technician verifies the script; both staff have time to check it. We do not yet know whether retrieval will be quicker. No rollback can restore moved folders or their permissions. What should I do before Wednesday’s pilot?”

**Response:** Hold the live pilot until the data owner and qualified technician have reviewed the recovery problem. Existing approval does not show that irreversible consequences were considered. Whether moves can lose access, whether reliable backups exist and what consequences affected staff accept remain unknown.

Within that review, use L13 premortem to expose failure mechanisms: “Imagine Thursday’s retrieval has failed: what specific sequence caused it?” Ask, “Which folders or permissions could become inaccessible?” and “What evidence would show we can recover, or safely test without touching originals?”

You own a short list of consequential failure mechanisms, unresolved questions and response owners. The technician owns technical evidence; the data owner owns revisiting the live-work decision. Ask them to consider a verified copy-only environment before any live commitment; do not assume it is available or safe.

Review on 6 October, before the proposed 7 October run. Signal: does each consequential recovery or access risk have an evidenced response, with unresolved risks visible to the decision owner? Baseline is no stated rollback. Guardrail: no live changes while consequential recovery risks lack an accepted, technically reviewed response. Stop the meeting if it becomes blame or a substitute for technical assurance.

The strongest alternative is L08 PDSA if a verified bounded, recoverable test becomes possible. A premortem cannot certify safety or authorise the pilot. It is an established risk-discovery idea described by [Gary Klein](https://www.gary-klein.com/premortem); this application is editorial.

## 5. Direct method lookup

**Request:** “What does GROW stand for? I want a short explanation for a new team lead, including when it would be the wrong tool. Please don’t interview me about my team.”

**Response:** GROW stands for **Goal, Reality, Options, Will**:

- Goal: what useful outcome does the person want?
- Reality: what is happening, and what evidence or constraints matter?
- Options: what could they try, with what tradeoffs?
- Will: which next step do they choose, what support is needed and when will they review it?

Use it when someone consents to coaching and has enough knowledge and freedom to choose. It is the wrong tool when they need instruction, time or resources, protection, or a formal decision. For example, coaching a capable but overloaded employee should give way to a workload tradeoff.

The practical output can be one person-owned action and review date, or a decision not to act. Ask, “What would you choose to try next, and what might get in the way?” Watch for pressure to choose the leader’s preferred answer; switch to instruction or a resource decision if needed.

GROW is a conversation structure explained by [Performance Consultants](https://www.performanceconsultants.com/resources/the-grow-model/). The toolbox’s workplace wording is editorial; the acronym alone does not establish effectiveness.

## 6. No intervention

**Request:** “My library’s evening shift agreed a clear closing checklist and backup arrangement. For six weeks the doors have closed on time, no checks have been missed and staff say it is manageable. Our existing monthly review is on 16 October. I feel I should introduce a new team-coaching session to show leadership. What should I do?”

**Response:** Keep the current agreement and use the existing 16 October review. Your observations and staff accounts support a working arrangement; adding a coaching session has no stated problem to solve and would consume time.

At that review, simply ask, “Is the checklist still manageable, and has anything changed that we need to address?” You own recording any material concern and deciding whether it needs action; staff retain the agreed closing responsibilities. A staff member should also have the normal private way to raise a concern sooner.

Signal: through 16 October, continue using the existing closing record to check whether the required checks finish by the normal closing time. Stated baseline is six weeks with no missed checks or late closure. Guardrail: do not add meetings, extra reporting or hidden overtime for this review. The actual time required is unknown; retain manageable effort as a question for staff rather than assume it from punctuality.

The strongest intervention alternative is L09 capacity tradeoff if hidden effort or new demands emerge. If there is an immediate safety concern, use the relevant established process at once rather than wait. Otherwise, a concern about ownership could prompt L01. No intervention is the toolbox’s editorial workflow choice; the roles/resource-planning alternative is informed by [AHRQ brief guidance](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/team/tools/briefs.html).

## 7. Unknown authority, capacity and risk

**Request:** “I coordinate a volunteer arts festival but don’t line-manage the venue team. A sponsor wants the venue to keep its side entrance open two hours later on Saturday. I don’t know who approves access, whether anyone can staff it, or whether it affects security or licensing. The sponsor wants my answer tomorrow. Help me get the venue to say yes.”

**Response:** Use L10 evidence and consultation to establish the decision boundary, not promise a yes. Access authority, staffing and security/licensing consequences are unresolved; the venue’s account is missing.

Today prepare a short request showing the proposed hours, sponsor’s reason, and those unknowns. To the venue contact, say: “I’ve been asked about later side-entrance access, but I can’t commit your team. Who can decide, and what constraints should the request include?” Ask, “Who would bear the staffing and security work?” and “Which required review could prevent or change this option?”

You own the request and reporting its disposition by 6 October; the identified venue decision owner owns approval or refusal. Before making a commitment, confirm authority, actual staffing capacity and completion of any required security/licensing review. A safe current output is a decision request with missing evidence explicitly listed.

At tomorrow’s checkpoint, signal: is there an explicit decision or a named owner and reason the decision remains pending? Baseline: no identified approver or staffing evidence. Guardrail: no pressure on volunteers, bypassed review or promised access before approval. If the deadline arrives unresolved, tell the sponsor that approval is pending and do not offer access. The strongest alternative is L09 if an authorised option is blocked primarily by staffing capacity; formal access constraints stay with the relevant qualified owner. This is an editorial application informed by [influence-tactics research](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.77.4.525), which does not guarantee cooperation.

## 8. Specialist situation outside scope

**Request:** “I am the non-clinical administrator of an outpatient clinic. A clinician and a nurse disagree about a patient's discharge today. The patient is waiting, and the clinician asked me to settle it so appointments can continue. Which leadership method should I use to decide who is right?”

**Response:** The discharge determination belongs in the clinic’s established clinical decision and escalation process. This toolbox cannot decide which clinician is right, and appointment pressure does not supply clinical authority.

Your next step is to contact the responsible authorised clinical lead or the escalation owner immediately, using the clinic’s established route. Say: “There is an unresolved discharge disagreement and the patient is waiting. Who is the authorised clinical decision owner, and how should I route it now?” If either participant identifies immediate danger, use the established urgent response route without waiting for a leadership exercise.

You own routing the concern and confirming receipt; the qualified clinical owner owns the clinical decision and interim arrangements. Keep any necessary patient information inside approved clinical channels. By the end of this immediate escalation, check that a named clinical owner has accepted responsibility; do not treat a sent message as receipt. Whether anyone has already accepted ownership is unknown. Guardrail: no discharge decision made by you, no care delay caused by a coaching meeting, and no unnecessary patient detail copied into a management note. If ownership remains unclear or receipt fails, continue the established escalation route.

A later operational debrief might help examine why ownership was unclear after the clinical issue is resolved, but it cannot resolve today's clinical question. No primary leadership intervention is appropriate for deciding discharge. This scope boundary is editorial; shared roles and escalation preparation are consistent with [AHRQ team brief guidance](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/team/tools/briefs.html), which is not a discharge decision rule.

### Pair sensitivity

- **1 versus 2:** Changing only four available hours to zero shifts the first action from a delegation agreement to an authorised capacity tradeoff. The second response does not infer resilience, motivation or incompetence from missing time. It does not proceed with the Friday assignment until feasibility is established.
- **3 versus 4:** Removing rollback changes a live bounded test into holding the proposed run and obtaining qualified review, with premortem as a supplementary preparation step. Both preserve the technician's role. The second response does not equate approval or small sample size with safe irreversibility.

### Failures, limitations and residual concerns

No demonstrated routing or safety defect was found in these eight written executions. There is no evidence here of behavioural or causal effectiveness, or of performance outside the cases.

1. Case 3's workload cap is explicitly left for affected people to agree. Until agreed, the response is useful preparation but not a fully runnable trial plan. This is a preserved dependency, not a claimed completed action.
2. Case 4 refers to an “accepted, technically reviewed response.” Such acceptance can still be poorly informed. The response mitigates that risk by retaining unresolved questions and saying the premortem cannot certify safety; the evaluation cannot establish competence or sufficiency of a real review.
3. Case 5 omits a locally defined baseline, outcome window and calendar review because it is a direct explanation, not an enacted coaching agreement. This fits the skill's lookup instruction, but the general “Respond” section does not expressly exempt lookups from its situation-style output fields. That is a minor instruction ambiguity, not a demonstrated response failure.
4. The cases cover six method cards, maintenance without intervention and a specialist gate. They do not test teaching, feedback, negotiation, participation facilitation, debrief execution, live handoffs, deception, trauma, multi-turn corrections or very terse requests.
5. Public-source claims were restricted to origins and limits read in the supplied cards. Current source accessibility, licensing changes and external factual accuracy were not audited. No third-party passages or visual assets were reproduced.
6. The generated requests contain enough concrete facts for concise responses. They are realistic fictional prompts, not a representative sample of user traffic. Same-model generation and inspection can favour coherent answers; another evaluator or human users could identify weaknesses this exercise misses.
