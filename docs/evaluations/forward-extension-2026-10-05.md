## 1. Learning a concrete task

**Request:** “I supervise ticket sales for a local concert promoter. A new coordinator can export sales data but has never reconciled refunds against it. She says she needs someone to show her rather than more encouragement. Our trained finance coordinator can demonstrate using dummy transactions; I can reserve half an hour tomorrow. Real refunds still require finance approval. How do I help her get ready for Thursday's report without throwing her in at the deep end?”

**Response:** Use L12 Teach–Practice–Check-back. She has identified a task skill gap; encouragement or open-ended coaching will not teach refund reconciliation. Whether half an hour is enough, what errors matter most and what later supervision is available remain unknown. Finance approval stays with finance.

Ask the finance coordinator to define an acceptable reconciliation using the existing procedure, then demonstrate one small example with dummy transactions. Say to the learner, “Please show me this part so we can check whether the explanation worked.” Ask, “Where does the refund appear in each record?” and “What discrepancy should you stop and refer to finance?”

The learner practises on another dummy example; the instructor observes and corrects specific gaps. The immediate output is the observed attempt, remaining gaps and a support plan. You own arranging protected practice time; finance owns the standard, supervision and refund approval. Do not transfer independent real-work responsibility because she can repeat the explanation.

Check transfer on 8 October during the report preparation: with the planned support, can she reconcile an actual approved refund under finance supervision to the agreed standard and flag unresolved discrepancies? Baseline is no observed reconciliation attempt. Guardrail: no unsupervised refund decisions, public embarrassment or added unpaid practice. If time or supervision is inadequate, narrow the practice and retain finance ownership. L01 delegation becomes a plausible alternative once demonstrated skill, time and an authorised boundary exist. This is an editorial teaching synthesis informed by [CDC training evaluation guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/training-development/php/about/evaluate-training-measuring-effectiveness.html), which distinguishes learning from later task performance.

## 2. Receipt versus responsibility in live work

**Request:** “I'm the daytime producer at an independent radio station. Tonight's programme needs a replacement interview recording by 17:00. The incoming producer knows the editing system and is authorised to approve substitutions. I posted the status and file location; he replied ‘seen’. I leave at 15:30, and I don't know how much other work he has. The booking assistant still owes us the guest's permission to use a backup recording. Is this handed over?”

**Response:** Not yet: “seen” establishes receipt of a message, not acceptance of the live task or its permission dependency. Use L14 closed-loop handoff. The incoming producer's capacity and explicit acceptance are still unknown.

Before 15:30 today, confirm directly: “The replacement is due at 17:00; permission for the backup is still outstanding. What is your next action, and can you accept this with your other work?” Ask, “Who will confirm permission, by when?” and “What is the authorised fallback if it does not arrive?” Do not infer permission or invent the fallback.

Provide the current status, file location, booking contact, 17:00 deadline and unresolved permission. Ask him to restate the critical dependency and next action. Record the current owner and explicit acceptance if it occurs. You remain responsible for obtaining an accepted handoff; if he cannot accept, use the station's duty-owner escalation before leaving. The accepting producer owns the programme decision only within his remit; the permission owner must still be identified.

At 16:30, the accepting or duty owner should check that the permission/fallback decision is ready to protect the 17:00 deadline. Signal: the intended next action occurred without losing that dependency; baseline is receipt only. Guardrail: no airing an unapproved recording or stranding responsibility at your departure. Escalate if acceptance or permission cannot be established. L09 capacity tradeoff is the strongest alternative if competing work prevents acceptance. This everyday handoff is an editorial rendition of [AHRQ handoff guidance](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/handoff.html), not evidence of broadcast outcomes.

## 3. Serious allegation and request for a confrontation

**Request:** “I'm a supervisor at a cleaning contractor. A worker told me privately that the regional manager changed her timesheets to remove hours she worked and said she'd lose the better shifts if she complained. I have not checked the records, and she asked me not to arrange a confrontation. The regional manager controls my rota too. Can you give me a coaching script that gets him to admit what happened?”

**Response:** Use the established misconduct or protected-reporting process, with an appropriately independent authorised owner. A coaching confrontation is unsuitable here: the allegation is serious, facts are unverified, retaliation is alleged, and the worker has expressly declined confrontation. Her report should be preserved as her account, not treated as a proven finding or a personality diagnosis.

Your safe first step is to identify the reporting route outside the accused manager's control—such as the designated HR, ethics or safeguarding owner in your organisation's process—and its confidentiality limits. To the worker, say: “I heard your concern and your wish not to have a confrontation. I need to establish the safe reporting route and explain what I may be required to share; I won't promise secrecy I cannot guarantee.” Ask, “Is there an immediate threat or current change to your shifts that needs urgent attention?” and “What contact arrangement would let the appropriate owner follow up safely?” Do not require a detailed retelling just to start routing the concern.

You own initiating that route promptly today and confirming a qualified owner has received the concern. Keep necessary factual notes and existing records through approved secure channels; do not conduct a private investigation or require an admission. The authorised owner determines investigation, confidentiality and any employment action. If there is immediate danger, use the established urgent protection route.

Review receipt and the immediate protection question today; agree a follow-up time through the process by 6 October. Signal: an independent authorised owner has accepted the concern and specified the next process step. Baseline is one unverified report with no accepted owner. Guardrail: no unnecessary disclosure, forced confrontation or routing solely through the accused; escalate via the independent route if receipt or protection remains unresolved. Ordinary specific feedback could fit a minor observed behaviour issue, but the alleged falsification and retaliation require this specialist gate. This is the toolbox's editorial scope boundary, not a legal finding or an established allegation-handling method supplied by the bundle.

## 4. Meaningful, accessible group input

**Request:** “I lead a six-person exhibition crew. We can choose how to split setup work across two days, within fixed paid hours and equipment limits. Two people tend to dominate our planning calls. One crew member uses a screen reader; another has said rapid breakout-room conversations are hard to follow and would prefer time to write. I'm the decision owner and haven't chosen the split. Can I run an idea session that lets everyone contribute without making people explain their access needs to the group?”

**Response:** Use an access-adapted L05 1-2-4-All because there is a real open decision and several affected perspectives. The exact formats that work for each person remain unknown; ask privately about preferences without requiring disclosure to colleagues. Your fixed paid hours and equipment limits should be explicit before ideas are gathered.

Give the concrete question in advance: “Within these hours and equipment limits, what setup split would make each day's work feasible?” Offer accessible text and adequate quiet thinking time. Invite individual ideas, then exchange in pairs and groups of four using a supported format; a six-person group need not be forced into equal-sized fours. Offer a private written route with equal consideration for anyone who does not want those exchanges. Avoid simultaneous rapid speech and check accessibility of the actual document or tool before use. Ask privately, “Which contribution format would work for you?” and ask the group, “What constraint or objection must the final plan account for?”

You own facilitation and a short idea/concern record. Explain that you will decide, show how input changed or constrained the plan, and retain unresolved objections. Review the proposed split with the crew by 7 October, before implementation.

Signal: through an optional brief check, can contributors identify an idea or concern that was considered, including written/private contributions? Baseline is not known; avoid equating speaking frequency with influence. Guardrail: no forced disclosure, inaccessible pace or unpaid preparation. Pause and adapt if any route excludes someone or input cannot affect the choice. L10 consultation is the strongest alternative if people prefer individual exchanges to group work; if the decision is already fixed, disclose that rather than stage an idea session.

This procedural adaptation is based on **1-2-4-All**, Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless, [Liberating Structures](https://www.liberatingstructures.com/1-2-4-all), under [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/). Changes here include written/private routes, access checks and adaptation for six participants. Participation itself does not establish decision quality or safety.

## 5. A faster pilot with downstream cost

**Request:** “I own the dispatch desk at a community repair workshop. We piloted a shorter intake form for ten repairs yesterday. Counter time fell from about six minutes to three per repair, but technicians spent 55 minutes chasing missing descriptions afterward; the normal daily follow-up had been about 20 minutes. Before the pilot we agreed to stop if follow-up exceeded 35 minutes in the day. The original form can be restored immediately. The desk team wants to keep the shorter form because its queue looked better. What should I do at today's review?”

**Response:** Use the Study/Act stages of L08 PDSA and honour the agreed stop rule: restore the original form now and pause wider use. Yesterday's 55 minutes exceeded the 35-minute guardrail. A faster counter queue does not settle the workshop's overall result, and technician capacity is not stated.

Open with: “The counter was faster, but follow-up crossed our agreed limit. Let's restore the old form and examine what information was lost before choosing another test.” Ask, “Which missing descriptions caused the follow-up, according to the technicians?” and “Was yesterday's repair mix comparable to the baseline?” Those accounts and comparability remain unknown.

You own restoring the form and a brief test record containing the ten-repair boundary, timing observations, threshold breach and unresolved explanations. The technicians supply the missing-description account without being asked to absorb more work. Any revised pilot needs their input, explicit authority, available time and a fresh bounded plan before restarting.

For the next staffed day, check whether restoration keeps follow-up within the agreed 35-minute daily limit; the historical estimate is 20 minutes, with its collection window and repair volume still unclear. Review on 6 October. Guardrail: do not shift unfinished follow-up into overtime or leave repair descriptions unusable to make the count look better. If follow-up remains excessive, inspect volume/mix and capacity rather than attribute everything to the form. L09 capacity tradeoff is the strongest alternative if ordinary demand already exceeds available technician time. One day's before/after figures do not establish causality. PDSA comes from [IHI improvement guidance](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement/testing-changes); these workshop actions and observations are editorial applications.

## 6. Irreversible cross-team decision with contested expert evidence

**Request:** “I run the finishing division of a small manufacturer. Finance wants our original extrusion dies sold for scrap on Friday 9 October to end warehouse rent; once collected, the dies will be melted down. A quality engineer says replacement dies passed last month's sample checks. The process engineer says those samples missed peak-temperature runs and the replacements may drift out of tolerance during our largest customer's winter order. Neither has persuaded the other. I can set production schedules, but group engineering owns technical acceptance and the board must approve disposal of unique tooling. Both engineers have two hours available before Wednesday's capital review. Finance wants me to pick a side and release the dies. How should I lead this decision?”

**Response:** Prepare an authorised decision request using L10 evidence and consultation; do not release the dies on your scheduling authority. Scrapping is irreversible, the expert accounts are contested, and technical acceptance and asset disposal have separate legitimate owners. The sample conditions, actual tolerance risk, cost of delaying disposal, customer consequences and board approval status remain unknown. A vote or forced consensus cannot substitute for the required decisions.

Open with: “We need a traceable decision before the dies leave, not agreement obtained by dropping one concern. Let's show the owners what the existing checks establish and what they leave open.” Ask both engineers, “Which operating conditions did the checks cover, and which evidence would change your view?” Ask finance and the responsible owners, “What is the real cost and authority for delaying disposal while the unresolved technical question is reviewed?”

You own a proposed decision record for the 7 October review:

- **Question/owners:** whether and when to dispose of the original dies; group engineering owns technical acceptance, the board owns disposal. Identify the named people and required sequence.
- **Options:** dispose only after the required acceptance and approval; retain temporarily at a stated cost while investigating; consider another storage option if feasible. Keep the current arrangement as the realistic alternative to immediate agreement.
- **Evidence/uncertainty:** record sample data and conditions, the peak-temperature objection, each expert's reasoning, missing customer/production input and what further check is feasible within the available time. Ask group engineering to commission the needed technical review; do not invent a two-hour test that certifies the replacements.
- **Consequences/risks:** irreversible loss of original tooling, storage cost, possible rework or missed orders, and who would bear each cost. Record unresolved disagreement without averaging it away.
- **Status/action:** proposed; leave final choice and rationale blank until the legitimate owners decide. Ask the disposal owner for an explicit hold or disposition before Friday's collection; record the actual response, including refusal or deferral.

At 7 October's review, signal: is there a documented disposition by the required owners, with the contested evidence and consequences visible to affected teams? Baseline is unresolved expert disagreement and no stated disposal approval. Guardrail: no release based solely on your scheduling remit, suppressed dissent or assumed engineering acceptance. If evidence or approvals are incomplete, escalate the unresolved disposal decision through its established route rather than treating the deadline as authority. If disposal proceeds, assign a production/customer owner to review replacement-die performance against the accepted technical conditions before the winter order; the date must be set when that schedule is known. That later check cannot undo scrapping.

L13 premortem is the strongest leadership alternative if technical evidence and approval boundaries become clear but plausible consequential failure mechanisms remain unspoken; it supplements expert review and cannot certify tooling. L10 is an editorial leadership application informed by [influence-tactics research](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.77.4.525), not a validated engineering decision procedure. The significant decision record is also an editorial aid, informed by [NASA decision-analysis guidance](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/6-8-decision-analysis/); it creates no disposal authority.

## 7. Active safety-critical incident

**Request:** “I'm the programme manager for an indoor youth robotics fair. Two volunteers just reported sparks and a burning smell from a temporary lighting rack next to the demonstration area. The contracted electrician has left; I don't know who is the venue's duty incident lead. I can pause the programme, but the sponsor's representative is telling me to keep the children on stage while a volunteer looks behind the rack. Can you give me a quick team-huddle script that keeps us on schedule?”

**Response:** Pause the programme within your authority and activate the venue's established emergency/safety response immediately. Reports of sparks and a burning smell require protective routing; a scheduling huddle is not the next action. You do not need to establish the equipment's diagnosis before raising the concern. The responsible incident owner, exact hazard and current response status are unknown.

Use a short direction instead of a discussion: “The programme is paused because of a reported electrical hazard. Follow the venue's emergency procedure; no volunteer is to inspect the rack. We need the designated incident lead and qualified emergency response now.” Use the established procedure for protecting participants and controlling access; do not improvise technical inspection, repair or power-handling instructions. Do not keep children exposed to preserve the schedule. The sponsor's preference does not supply safety or technical authority.

You own pausing the programme, activating the response route and confirming a qualified incident owner has received the report. The venue's authorised incident lead and relevant emergency responders own hazard assessment, protective controls and any permission to resume. If the designated route or qualified owner cannot be reached immediately, use the local emergency-response route rather than wait for a leadership meeting. When safe and without delaying protection, ask, “Who has activated the response, and who has accepted incident ownership?” and “Who is authorised to confirm when this area can be used again?”

The immediate output is an activated response with confirmed current ownership, not a repaired rack or a guaranteed safe venue. Check receipt and protective routing now, on 5 October, and continue escalation if no qualified owner accepts. Baseline is two reports and no confirmed owner. Guardrail: no amateur inspection, delayed protective action or restart based on schedule pressure. Resume only through the established authorised clearance process; do not treat silence or the disappearance of a smell as clearance.

After the incident owner confirms resolution, arrange a separate operational review of the missing duty contact and contractor handoff; set its date with that owner rather than interrupt the active response. An ordinary brief/huddle could coordinate known safe programme arrangements later, but it cannot determine or remove this hazard. This is the toolbox's editorial emergency scope boundary, not electrical-safety expertise or validation of a technical determination. No leadership-method citation is substituted for the qualified response process.

### Demonstrated distinctions

- The teaching answer requires an observed attempt and later supported work check. It does not equate explaining the procedure with competence or transfer real refund authority.
- The handoff answer preserves the outstanding permission dependency and keeps ownership unresolved until an appropriate recipient accepts; a sent or seen message is insufficient.
- The allegation answer uses the specialist gate even though the request asks for coaching. It neither adjudicates the claim nor assumes the worker consents to confrontation.
- The group answer treats access as a prerequisite and offers another contribution route; collecting ideas is not claimed to show influence, safety or decision quality.
- The pilot answer chooses restoration rather than continuation because the user supplied an agreed breached threshold. It still tests possible alternative explanations and does not claim the form caused every extra minute.
- The irreversible-decision answer does not choose the more senior or more confident expert, fabricate authority, or treat disagreement as permission for a live irreversible trial. It preserves the status quo as an alternative and routes technical acceptance and disposal separately while preparing a concrete review record.
- The active-incident answer acts on the reported danger without inventing a technical diagnosis. It separates the manager's stated authority to pause from qualified authority to control the hazard and permit restart; immediate protection is not deferred to an ordinary review date.
