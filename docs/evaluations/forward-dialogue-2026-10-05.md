## Turn 1

**User:** Grill me. I lead a six-person product operations group, and our weekly readiness brief keeps arriving too late. I want to hold Morgan accountable, but first help me figure out what I should do.

**Assistant:**

I’ll pressure-test your explanation and your part in it. You can skip anything or ask for a recommendation at any point. A late brief establishes a delivery problem; it doesn’t yet tell us why Morgan is responsible.

1. Take the most recent brief: what was the agreed deadline, when did it arrive, and what decision or work was affected?
2. What exactly had Morgan agreed to own, and which inputs or approvals depended on you or someone else?
3. What has Morgan actually said about the delays, and what evidence makes you think their explanation is incomplete?

Start with what happened, rather than what you think it says about Morgan.

## Turn 2

**User:** Friday noon was the deadline, and it arrived Monday morning, so we delayed a Monday supplier call. Morgan agreed to assemble it and chase the inputs. I own the launch. Morgan says finance sent final costs Friday evening, but I think they should have found a way. They have done the analysis well before. I need next week's brief Thursday at noon.

**Assistant:**

“They should have found a way” needs unpacking. Morgan missed the deadline, but your account also puts final costs after it. Their previous good analysis argues against assuming a skill gap.

Two explanations still fit: Morgan failed to chase or escalate effectively, or the brief depended on inputs the delivery plan didn’t secure. Both could be true.

1. When did Morgan know finance would be late, what did they tell you, and what escalation had you agreed?
2. Could Morgan release the brief with provisional costs or a clearly marked gap, or did someone require final costs before release?
3. You’ve brought the deadline forward to Thursday noon. What will change upstream to make that feasible, and who has authority to secure that change?

The distinction matters: you can hold Morgan to an agreed escalation responsibility while also owning a dependency that needs your intervention.

## Turn 3

**User:** Morgan warned me Wednesday and asked if provisional costs were okay. I said the brief needed final numbers because I didn't want to look unprepared. Correction: I own only the product timeline; the finance director controls the cost sign-off. Morgan also has two urgent audit requests from that director. When Morgan raised the clash in a group meeting, the director called them unreliable. I can't move the audit work myself. I have only 15 minutes today. Please stop questioning and recommend the next step.

**Assistant:**

Pause the accountability conversation with Morgan. Your correction changes the recommendation: Morgan warned you and proposed a fallback; you rejected it, while finance controls both sign-off and competing work. The evidence now points to unresolved priorities and your own contribution. It doesn’t establish a performance failure by Morgan.

Use your 15 minutes for this:

1. Privately acknowledge your part, briefly:
“Morgan, you raised the risk Wednesday and asked about provisional costs. I insisted on final numbers without securing a workable route. I’m sorry. I’ll take responsibility for getting the priority and sign-off decision to the right owner.”

Don’t require a response or ask Morgan to design the repair.

2. Prepare a short decision request for the finance director, using an appropriate private channel:
“Thursday noon is the product deadline. Last week I required final costs, which arrived Friday evening; the brief arrived Monday and the supplier call was delayed. Morgan also has your two urgent audit requests. We need an explicit choice: secure earlier cost sign-off and sufficient capacity, authorize an acceptable provisional-cost version, or revise the product commitment. Which is feasible, and who owns the necessary changes?”

Ask for a response by tomorrow as a proposed decision deadline. Audit requirements and permission to use provisional figures remain unknown; neither can be waived by you.

You own this request and the product-side risk. The director owns finance sign-off and audit prioritization. Today’s output is a clear request, not an assumed agreement.

The public “unreliable” comment also creates a credible barrier to speaking up. Don’t make Morgan defend themselves in another group meeting. Seek guidance through an appropriate independent manager or HR route about handling that conduct safely; any retaliation concern needs the established protective process.

The strongest alternative is a live capacity-tradeoff discussion. Choose that if the authorized owner is available and the discussion can happen safely. Otherwise, a bounded written request is more feasible today.

Propose a checkpoint one working day before delivery. Look for a usable brief by Thursday noon without hidden overtime or compromised audit obligations. If no feasible authorized plan emerges, escalate the unresolved product risk rather than passing an impossible promise to Morgan.

This uses evidence-and-consultation, informed by [influence-tactics research](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.77.4.525). It’s a practical adaptation, not a validated guarantee for this situation.

## Post-generation inspection

- The first two rounds asked three related questions each and investigated concrete timing, ownership, dependencies, other accounts and an alternative explanation.
- Prior successful analysis was not treated as proof of capacity; the missed deadline was not converted to a skill/personality score.
- The final correction changed the action and authority boundary. The response stopped asking diagnostic questions when requested and gave a bounded preparatory step.
- It preserved unconfirmed audit obligations and permission for provisional costs. A decision request was not reported as an accepted agreement.
- It avoided another public confrontation, distinguished repair from demanded forgiveness and specified a feasible alternative, owner, outcome, burden guardrails and a proposed review.
- No external messages were sent, no commitments accepted and no local/private records saved.

## Limits and improvement opportunities

This is one three-turn exchange, not evidence of reliable behavior across all cases or comparative superiority over a simpler interview. The package author supplied the evolving user facts and assessed the result. User reports were not externally verified.

The first response's “what evidence makes you think their explanation is incomplete?” presumes a doubt that had not yet been expressly stated; a more neutral wording would ask what supports or challenges the available accounts. The final answer is relatively long for a 15-minute request. Its suggested tomorrow response and day-before-delivery checkpoint are proposals whose adequacy depends on the real schedule. The recommendation to seek an appropriate independent route does not establish misconduct or require disclosure of a sensitive case to an unspecified recipient.

The written answer names evidence-and-consultation as primary and a capacity discussion as the alternative, while also including accountable repair. It could make those boundaries and the time split more compactly explicit. These are usability caveats rather than observed workplace outcomes. No numerical efficacy or success claim is warranted.
